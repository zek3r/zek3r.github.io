# The Two-Way Interaction Between STEM and Society
It is obvious that there is a two-way interaction between STEM (Science, Tech, Engineering, Mathematics) and systemic–societal and political–forces. This is perhaps most clear when we, as STEM researchers, fill out grants and scholarships: we motivate work based on how discoveries are going to improve things like the economy, health, and security, and our work is in turn selected according to how it fills roles that are prioritized according to societal norms. 

What might be less considered is how this two-way interaction can often end up shaping STEM endeavours and applications so that they cause harm. This interaction can be seen in the integration of STEM and the U.S. military, where research is often funded by DARPA and its fruit is put to use in, e.g., guidance and avionics systems used in mass bombings of civilian populations (e.g. Vietnam, Afghanistan, Iraq, Gaza)1. The interaction can again be seen in recent literature suggesting that tech advancements–increased automation, information and communication technologies (e.g. the internet)–perhaps counterintuitively correlate with increased wealth and income inequality2. Given the use of AI in defence and workplace automation, coupled with the proximity of my work to AI, for me these examples hit close to home.

## Why Write on This Topic?

The way in which technology and science are shaped and subverted by an array of systemic forces is actually quite well studied in the arts and humanities3. So why am I devoting (virtual) ink to it? Because, in my experience, this topic is not commonly considered in popular discourse nor even by STEM researchers (with exceptions, of course!).

If the subversion of STEM by systemic forces was more often considered by scientists, technologists, and the public, we, as a society, might be able to better wield the formidable power of STEM not only for the benefit of a select wealthy few–be they politicians ordering bombing runs or investors raking in record profits–but for a more global benefit. Conversely, without careful reflection upon systemic factors, even the most benevolently crafted technological innovations and scientific theories may be subverted or, like a paywalled vaccine, rendered ineffective.


## My Objectives for these SySTEMic Blog Posts

The goal of my interweaving of posts about systemic and political forces into my blog is twofold: (1) to give me a reason to educate myself so as to better understand the place of my science in the world; (2) to provide an accessible, bite-sized resource that friends and colleagues in STEM can use if they want to consider such systemic interactions in their own work, or even just understand the non-STEM world a little better. Ultimately, I hope that this will open up more of a discussion around these topics.

## Important Definitions

As an applied mathematician I am used to beginning an endeavour with definitions, so I’m going to wrap up today’s post by defining three key concepts that will appear frequently in my socio-political blog posts. Note that the following concepts are obviously quite nuanced, so saying that the definitions that I provide will be incomplete is an understatement. But–much like a mathematical model of a complex system will almost never fully describe the system and yet can be used to draw insightful conclusions–I hope that these definitions will set a foundation that you can use to better understand my future posts.

### Capitalism

There is much academic debate over the definition of capitalism. At an introductory level one can think of it as follows: an economic system where the very wealthy, the capitalists, own the means of production, the infrastructure required to make stuff, and use their position of power to solicit paid labour (referred to as wage labour) from the rest of society with the goal of deriving profit. 

It is worth mentioning a few key characteristics of capitalism. First, capital accumulation, which refers to the fact that, under capitalism, the point of possessing capital–money, resources–is not to possess and maintain but to grow. When a capitalist invests their money they do so with the intent of receiving a return that is greater than their original investment. This desire for growth leads to a second key aspect: competitive markets. Capitalists compete with each other to own more and more, while other entities, such as businesses, must compete to gain investment from capitalists. In this way capitalists decide what gets invested in, and these decisions are dictated primarily based on what is considered profitable. This leads to a third core characteristic, which is the maximization of exchange-value–how much a product can be sold for–rather than use-value–how much benefit a product brings the consumer.

It is equally worth mentioning some common misconceptions about capitalism. First, while certain capitalist countries, e.g. U.S., are sometimes viewed as classless, capitalism is innately divided into at least two classes: the capitalists, who own the means of production, and the workers, who sell their wage labour to the capitalists. Because there are a lot more workers, if you live under capitalism you are likely not a capitalist yourself. E.g., as I cannot stop working and survive off of capital returns I myself am a worker and, statistically, so is everyone reading this blog. As workers, the wages that we make in our jobs are qualitatively different from the profit that capitalists make: our salaries are derived from our labour while the capitalist’s profit does not require the capitalist to work. Instead, the capitalist’s profit is a portion of the value that the workers produce, taken as a levy. Lastly, it bears mentioning that capitalism is not characterized by the existence of markets or money, as these have existed since time immemorial while capitalism has only existed since, roughly, the 1600s.

### Imperialism \& Colonialism

I am defining these together because they are often conflated and, while the difference between them is not cut-and-dried in the literature, it is clear that they are closely related. Both imperialism and colonialism are concepts to describe the domination of a group of people by another, the latter usually being a state power. It seems that colonialism denotes the set of practices associated with domination of ‘group A’ by ‘group B’ through colonization–the dispossession of A’s land and the assigning of it to people from B. Imperialism, on the other hand, refers to the entire set of ideologies, theories, and practices associated with the domination of A by–a typically physically distant–B. For example, European domination of North America by establishing English and French settlements is colonization, an act of colonialism, whereas U.S. domination of South and Central American countries (e.g. Chili, Venezuela, Guatemala) by economic means and/or overthrowing governments represents imperialism.
